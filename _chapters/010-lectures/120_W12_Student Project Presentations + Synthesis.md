# Project Presentations and Course Synthesis

The final week has two purposes: to **present and discuss** your projects, and to **step back** and connect the individual weeks into a single way of thinking about landscapes. By now you have met systems thinking, terrain analysis, river profiles, tectonic geomorphology, χ analysis, the stream power model, landscape evolution models, hillslope–channel coupling, transience, and uncertainty. Here we assemble them.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this session you should be able to:
- communicate a quantitative geomorphic analysis clearly and critically,
- give and receive constructive scientific feedback,
- connect process laws, model behaviour, and field observations into one coherent picture,
- articulate what quantitative geomorphology can and cannot tell us.

---

# Part 1 – Student Project Presentations

## Format
- **10–12 minutes** per presentation + 3–5 minutes questions.
- Aim for the structure of a short conference talk, not a report read aloud.

## Suggested talk structure
1. **Motivation** — the landscape/region and the question. Why does it matter?
2. **Data and methods** — DEM(s), tools, metrics, or model setup used.
3. **Results** — the key figures (profiles, χ-plots, kₛₙ maps, model output).
4. **Interpretation** — what the results mean for tectonics, climate, or process.
5. **Uncertainty** — limitations, alternative explanations, equifinality.
6. **Take-home message** — one sentence the audience should remember.

## Assessment criteria
| Criterion | What we look for |
|-----------|------------------|
| Scientific question | Clear, focused, motivated |
| Methods | Appropriate, correctly applied, reproducible |
| Figures | Legible, labelled, self-explanatory |
| Interpretation | Linked to process; goes beyond description |
| Critical thinking | Acknowledges uncertainty and alternatives |
| Delivery | Clear, within time, handles questions |

## Peer feedback
Every student gives structured feedback on at least one talk:
- **One strength** — what worked and why.
- **One question** — something genuinely unclear or worth probing.
- **One suggestion** — a concrete way to strengthen the analysis.

> [!NOTE]
> The goal of feedback is to improve the science, not to grade the speaker. Be specific, be kind, be useful.

# Part 2 – Course Synthesis

## The single idea
Everything in this course is one loop:

> **Forcing → process laws → topographic signal → inversion back to forcing.**

Tectonics and climate impose boundary conditions; process laws (incision, diffusion, mass wasting) convert them into topography; we then read topography *backwards* to infer the forcing — always imperfectly.

## Recurring concepts
- **Conservation of mass** as the master equation: $\partial z / \partial t = U - E - D$.
- **Steady state vs. transience** — landscapes are usually adjusting, not equilibrated.
- **Thresholds and magnitude–frequency** — not all work is done by everyday events.
- **Signal vs. noise** — topography records forcing, but lithology, inheritance, and stochasticity blur it.
- **Equifinality** — different histories can produce the same landform; agreement is not proof.

## Key relationships, in one place
- Stream power incision: $E = K\,A^{m}\,S^{n}$
- Channel steepness and concavity: $S = k_{s}\,A^{-\theta}$
- Hillslope diffusion: $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial t} = D\,\nabla^{2} z$
- Slope stability (infinite slope): $FoS = \dfrac{\text{resisting forces}}{\text{driving forces}}$

## Big questions revisited
- Can two landscapes with the same uplift rate look completely different? *(Yes — why?)*
- How long does a tectonic or climatic signal take to appear in topography?
- When is a river profile telling us about **tectonics**, and when about **lithology** or **climate**?
- What does it mean for a model to "agree" with data — and what does it *not* mean?
- Where are the biggest uncertainties in everything we did this semester?

## Where to go next
- **Methods to deepen:** cosmogenic ¹⁰Be erosion rates, thermochronology, high-resolution (lidar/SfM) topography.
- **Tools to explore:** TopoToolbox, LSDTopoTools, fuller Landlab models.
- **Frontiers:** coupling climate and tectonics, sediment flux and the "tools and cover" effect, biota as a geomorphic agent, the Anthropocene.

> [!TIP]
> The most valuable thing you take from this course is not any single metric or model — it is the habit of asking *"what process could produce this form, and how would I test it?"*

## Closing
Quantitative geomorphology does not replace looking at landscapes; it disciplines it. You now have the vocabulary and tools to read topography as a record of Earth-surface processes — and, just as importantly, to know where that record is silent or ambiguous.
