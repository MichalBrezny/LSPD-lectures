# Introduction: Why Tectonics Matters

>*The unrelenting competition between tectonic processes that tend to build topography and surface processes that tend to tear them down represents the core of tectonic geomorphology.* 
>D.W. Burbank, R.S. Anderson, Tectonic Geomorphology (2011)

Tectonics is a primary driver of landscape evolution. It sets the long-term and large-scale boundary conditions within which surface processes operate. Tectonic processes raise rock above the base level and so create **topographic gradients**, and these gradients drive river incision, hillslope transport and mass wasting. Where rock uplift is faster, relief and slopes are higher and erosion is more intensive. Tectonics also controls the spatial pattern of erosion and deposition and the overall morphology of the landscape.

Erosion alone can only redistribute and ultimately reduce relief. Thus:
- Landscapes do not generate relief on their own in the long term.
- The production of relief requires **tectonic forcing**:
  - crustal shortening and thickening,
  - faulting and folding,
  - isostatic adjustment.

Tectonic geomorphology works in both directions. We use the knowledge of tectonics to explain landforms, and we use landforms to detect and measure tectonic deformation where it cannot be observed directly.

## Types of Tectonic Forcing

### Vertical Motions
The word "uplift" is used for three different things, and it is essential to distinguish them (England and Molnar, 1990):

- **Rock uplift** is the vertical motion of rock relative to a fixed reference level, the geoid.
- **Surface uplift** is the change in the elevation of the land surface, averaged over an area, relative to the geoid.
- **Exhumation** (denudation) is the motion of rock relative to the surface, i.e., the thickness of material removed from above it.

They are linked by a simple relationship:

$$ \text{surface uplift} = \text{rock uplift} - \text{exhumation} $$

If erosion removes material as fast as rock is uplifted, the surface elevation does not change, although the rock moves upwards all the time. This is what we call a steady-state landscape. Most methods measure only one of the three quantities: thermochronology and cosmogenic nuclides measure exhumation, a dated marine terrace measures rock uplift, and palaeoaltimetry measures surface uplift.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A high rate of exhumation or river incision is not evidence of surface uplift. It only shows that rock is moving towards the surface.

The mechanisms of vertical motion include:
- crustal thickening by shortening or magmatic addition,
- mantle dynamics (dynamic topography, removal of the lithospheric root),
- flexural and erosional isostasy.

Rates of rock uplift typically range from less than 0.01 mm/yr in stable continental interiors to several mm/yr in active mountain belts. The fastest rates, around 10 mm/yr, are known from places such as the Southern Alps of New Zealand or Taiwan.

The spatial pattern of uplift is as important as its rate. Uplift may be uniform over a large region, it may affect individual blocks, or it may be controlled by faults, with a gradient away from the fault.

### Isostasy
Isostasy is the gravitational equilibrium between the lithosphere and the underlying asthenosphere. The lithosphere floats on the denser mantle, and any change of the load on it is compensated by vertical movement: loading by ice, sediment or thrust sheets causes subsidence, and unloading by erosion or deglaciation causes uplift.

The consequence for erosion is important. If a layer of rock with the thickness $h$ is eroded over a large area, the isostatic rebound is

$$ \text{rebound} = \frac{\rho_c}{\rho_m} h \approx 0.8\,h $$

where $\rho_c \approx 2700\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ is the density of the crust and $\rho_m \approx 3300\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ is the density of the mantle. The removal of 1000 m of rock therefore lowers the mean surface elevation only by about 200 m, because about 800 m of rock rises from below to replace it. This is why mountain ranges persist for tens of millions of years after tectonic activity has stopped.

Isostasy responds to the *mean* erosion over a wide area. If rivers cut deep valleys while the summits are eroded slowly, the mean elevation decreases, but the summits can rise (Molnar and England, 1990).

### Horizontal Deformation
Most tectonic motion is horizontal, and vertical motions are often its by-product.
- **Shortening** produces fold-and-thrust belts with crustal thickening and uplift.
- **Extension** produces rift systems with subsiding basins and uplifted footwall blocks.
- **Strike-slip deformation** produces lateral offsets of landforms, pull-apart basins at releasing bends and uplift at restraining bends.

Horizontal deformation has direct consequences for drainage: rivers are deflected by growing structures, offset along strike-slip faults or captured, and basins are tilted (see the next lecture).

### Faulting, folding and earthquakes
On the scale of landforms, deformation is concentrated on faults and folds, which create local topographic highs and lows and influence drainage patterns and erosion rates. Displacement on faults accumulates mostly in earthquakes. A single event may produce a displacement of a few metres, and a mountain front several kilometres high is the result of thousands of such events. Long-term rates are therefore averages over many earthquake cycles.

## Geomorphic Responses to Tectonic Forcing

The response to tectonic forcing passes through the landscape in a fixed order: from rivers to hillslopes and to whole basins.

### Channel Response
Rivers respond first. Rock uplift raises the river bed relative to the base level, and the river has to incise to keep its position. A river adjusts mainly through its slope: it steepens until the incision rate equals the rate of rock uplift. In a river that is adjusted to uplift, steepness therefore reflects the uplift rate. A change in the uplift rate is not felt along the whole river at once. It starts at the downstream end and travels upstream as a knickpoint or a knickzone (W3). Rivers may also respond by narrowing their channels, which concentrates the erosive power on a smaller area of the bed.

### Hillslope Response
River incision lowers the base of the hillslopes and steepens them, and the relief grows. At low erosion rates, hillslopes respond by steepening, and their gradient increases with the erosion rate. Hillslopes cannot steepen without limit, however. When they reach the angle given by the strength of the rock, typically 30–35°, any further incision is compensated by landslides. Such **threshold hillslopes** keep the same gradient regardless of the erosion rate, and faster erosion is achieved by more frequent landslides and not by steeper slopes (Burbank et al., 1996). In rapidly uplifting mountains, mean hillslope gradient is therefore a poor indicator of the uplift rate, and channel steepness is a better one.

### Basin Response
On the scale of drainage basins, tectonics changes the drainage area and the geometry of the river network through tilting, divide migration and river capture. It also changes the sediment supply: faster uplift means more sediment and coarser sediment delivered to rivers and to the adjacent sedimentary basins.

### Transient Adjustment
The response is never immediate. There is a time lag between the change in uplift and the adjustment of the landscape, called the **response time**. For rivers in mountain ranges it is typically of the order of 10⁵ to 10⁷ years, depending on the size of the basin, the erodibility of rocks and the climate. Hillslopes cannot respond before the signal reaches the channel at their base, so the upper parts of a basin keep their old form the longest.

During this time the landscape is *transient*: it contains parts that are already adjusted to the new conditions and parts that still record the old ones. The landscape thus has a memory. What we observe today is a mixture of inherited topography and the response to the present forcing, and one of the main tasks of tectonic geomorphology is to separate the two.

## Conceptual Models

### Steady-State Orogen
If the tectonic forcing and climate stay constant for a time longer than the response time, the mountain range can reach a steady state, in which rock uplift is balanced by erosion (Willett and Brandon, 2002):
- the mean elevation and relief are constant, although individual landforms change (*topographic steady state*, see W1),
- rivers are in equilibrium with the tectonic forcing: they are graded and without migrating knickpoints,
- the erosion rate equals the rock uplift rate, so measured erosion rates can be read as uplift rates.

### Transient Orogen
After a change in the rate or pattern of uplift, the range is in a transient state:
- rivers steepen from their outlets and knickpoints migrate upstream,
- the erosion rate differs from the rock uplift rate, and the mean elevation changes,
- hillslopes adjust later than channels, and relict low-relief surfaces may survive in the headwaters.

### Limitations
Both models are idealizations, and three factors complicate their application:
- **Lithologic control**: resistant rocks produce steep channels and high relief without any difference in uplift.
- **Climate modulation**: precipitation changes the efficiency of erosion, and climate oscillates on timescales shorter than the response time, so a perfect steady state may never be reached.
- **Sediment cover**: sediment on the river bed can protect the bedrock from incision and delay the response.

## Wrap-up Discussion 
Key message:
> *Topography is a filtered tectonic signal.*

The landscape records tectonic forcing, but only after it has passed through the filter of surface processes, which delay it, smooth it and mix it with the signals of lithology and climate.

Discussion questions:
- Can two landscapes with the same uplift rate look different?
- How long does it take for tectonic signals to appear in topography?
- Which signals differentiate tectonic forcing from climatic forcing?

In the next lecture we will look at the tools for reading this signal: geomorphic markers, which record deformation directly, and geomorphic indices calculated from topography.
